# Track 1: The humanizer landscape

## Headline

1. The commercial humanizer market splits into two mechanical families, and
   neither helps us. Rule-based "word spinners" do one-to-one synonym
   replacement plus deliberate errors, spacing damage and non-standard Unicode.
   LLM-based humanizers are just another language model told to paraphrase "in
   a conversational tone". Both are designed to fool a classifier, not a reader.

2. The most important measured fact in the whole landscape is a tradeoff.
   Pangram found that the more readable a humanizer's output is, the more
   detectable it is. Fluent humanizers get caught; the only ones that slip
   through are the ones that turn prose into garbled, elementary-school
   nonsense. You pay for evasion in quality.

3. Detector robustness varies by an order of magnitude. On humanized text,
   Pangram detects 90 to 100 percent, GPTZero drops to 46 to 60 percent,
   Binoculars to 7 to 28 percent, FastDetectGPT to 23 percent. Copyleaks,
   ZeroGPT and QuillBot's own detector are trivially fooled (0 percent). So
   "beats the detectors" is not one claim; it depends entirely on which
   detector.

4. The people we actually care about, heavy LLM users who read a lot, still
   catch humanized text. Russell et al. found five such readers miss only 1
   of 300 humanized and paraphrased articles, beating every commercial and
   open-source detector they tested. The humanizer tax buys nothing against
   the reader who matters.

5. The unslop side has the one intervention with a clean, measured win:
   fine-tuning. The antislop fine-tune `sam-paech/gemma-3-27b-it-antislop`
   drops the EQ-Bench slop score from 69.5 to 20.8, near the human baseline of
   10.4. This is FTPO, the same intervention our own brief already ranked as
   the strongest with evidence, and it is open-weights only.

6. The best open-source "humanizers" are not humanizers at all. They are
   rule lists of structural tells (blader/humanizer, Wikipedia's signs) that
   describe what to remove, plus mechanical instruments (slop-forensics,
   EQ-Bench slop score, our own slopcheck) that count the tells. They target
   the reader and the defect, not the detector.

## Findings

### 1. What commercial humanizers actually do, mechanically

The best source on this is the Pangram team's own paper, DAMAGE
(arXiv:2501.03437, January 2025), which audited 19 humanizers and paraphrasers
and published a per-tool teardown. Pangram's non-academic writeup
("How well does Pangram perform on humanizers?", August 2025) summarizes the
mechanics in the same terms. Two families emerge.

**Rule-based word spinners.** These do not generate anything. They transform
the input text directly. The operations, in order of how often they appear:

- One-to-one synonym replacement. The classic example is "tortured phrases",
  a term from arXiv:2107.06751. "Artificial intelligence" becomes "counterfeit
  consciousness", "breast cancer" becomes "bosom peril". Pangram reports a
  student essay that rendered "Martin Luther King, Jr." as "Martin Luther
  Ruler, Jr." The signature is that the same tortured phrase reappears
  identically whenever the same source word occurs, which is also what gives
  it away.
- Deliberate spacing and punctuation errors. Humanizers add or remove spaces
  between sentences to disrupt the detector's tokenizer.
- Non-standard characters. Pangram names one popular humanizer that swaps the
  normal space for the thin space U+2009. Other tools insert zero-width
  spaces (U+200B) or zero-width joiners, or replace Latin letters with
  visually identical Cyrillic or Greek homoglyphs.
- Deliberate typos and degraded register. DAMAGE classifies Undetectable AI,
  WriteHuman.ai and Phrasly as tier 3 (worst) precisely because they write "at
  an elementary school level" and introduce typos. The errors are the product.

**LLM-based humanizers.** DAMAGE found that many humanizers are themselves
language models: either a fine-tuned LLM or a stock model with a system
prompt. They jailbroke one and recovered the prompt: "respond to the user
input with a reasonable approximation of the full meaning of the input... in a
conversational tone." Another was instructed to "write at a college level".
These tools rewrite whole sentences and paragraphs rather than swapping words,
which means they are weakly grounded: DAMAGE reports they add sentences that
were not in the original and delete others.

**DIPPER** (arXiv:2303.13408, NeurIPS 2023) is the academic ancestor of the
whole category: an 11B-parameter T5 paraphrase model with knobs for lexical
diversity and content reordering. It is the tool that originally demonstrated
paraphrasing evades detectors, dropping DetectGPT's detection accuracy from
70.3 percent to 4.6 percent at a constant 1 percent false positive rate.

**What this means for the surface-statistics question.** The two families
map cleanly onto two different attacks. Synonym swaps, spacing errors,
homoglyphs and zero-width characters all attack the *tokenizer and the
perplexity estimate*: they change the byte stream so the detector's model
sees different tokens than a reader sees. LLM paraphrasing attacks the
*distribution*: it re-rolls the text so the perplexity and burstiness
statistics shift. A homoglyph attack is the purest surface-statistics attack.
A COLING 2025 workshop paper (arXiv:2406.11239) measured it across seven
detectors and five datasets: average Matthews correlation dropped from 0.64
to -0.01, which is random. Perplexity-based detectors fell hardest;
classification-based detectors were more resilient but still degraded.

The key mechanical fact for us: **every one of these operations is aimed at
fooling a model, and every one degrades the text or leaves a new tell.**
Tortured phrases and hallucinated citations are themselves detectable, both by
Pangram's model and by eye.

### 2. Independent evaluations: do they beat detectors, and at what cost

The honest answer depends on which detector, and the spread is enormous. Three
independent benchmarks plus one vendor test give consistent numbers.

**Pangram's own numbers.** On a benchmark of 20 humanizers (August 2025),
Pangram reports accuracy from 90.3 percent (Undetectable AI) to 100 percent
(Grammarly, Quillbot, Ahrefs, Smodin, and others). On humanized academic text,
Pangram's DAMAGE model holds 98.3 percent true positive rate at 5 percent
false positive rate, against GPTZero's 60.0 percent and Binoculars' 28.2
percent (DAMAGE, Table 3). At the default threshold the contrast is sharper:
DAMAGE 97.5 percent, GPTZero 34.5 percent.

**Russell et al., ACL 2025** (arXiv:2501.15654). This is the independent study
Pangram cites. On humanized text, Pangram's best model reached 97 percent,
GPTZero 46 percent, FastDetectGPT 23 percent, Binoculars 7 percent. The same
paper's headline finding is that human readers who frequently use LLMs for
writing are better than all of them: five such annotators, majority vote,
misclassified 1 of 300 articles even under paraphrasing and humanization.

**Jabarian and Imas** (SSRN 5407424, cited by Pangram). Across four commercial
detectors under humanization, Pangram is the only robust one: false negative
rate near zero for long text. Originality.AI's FNR climbs to roughly 0.05 for
long text and up to 0.21 for short text. GPTZero "largely loses its capacity
to detect AI-generated text, with FNR scores around 0.50 and above." A RoBERTa
baseline does similarly poorly.

**GPTZero's own counter-test** (Uncheck AI review, 2026). GPTZero ran a
humanizer called Uncheck AI against six detectors. The humanized text scored
97 percent AI on GPTZero and 74 percent on Originality, but 0 percent on
Copyleaks, ZeroGPT and QuillBot, and 3 percent on Winston AI. Same text, six
detectors, a spread from fully caught to fully fooled.

**The cost, stated as a law.** Pangram's cleanest sentence is the one that
matters most for this project: "the more readable/fluent the humanizer text
is, the more likely it is to be detected." The tools that evade detection are
the ones that produce junk. DAMAGE quantifies the same thing from the quality
side with a Fluency Win Rate (how often GPT-4o prefers the humanized text over
the original): tier-1 humanizers win 26.0 percent, tier-2 win 14.67 percent,
tier-3 win 2.67 percent. "All humanizers tend to degrade the quality of the
original text." Even the best humanizer loses to its own input three times out
of four.

**Watermarking does not rescue the detector side either.** DAMAGE shows a
DIPPER paraphrase drops SynthID watermark detection from 87.6 percent to 5.4
percent true positive rate at 5 percent FPR. Watermarks are at least as
fragile as perplexity detectors.

### 3. Open-source humanizers and unslop tools

There is no meaningful open-source "humanizer" in the evasion sense. What
exists on the open side is the opposite: tools that *identify and remove* the
tells, aimed at quality rather than evasion.

**blader/humanizer** (github.com/blader/humanizer, ~53k stars, v3.1.0,
September 2026). A Claude Code skill that encodes 35 patterns taken from
Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing", plus an explicit no-invention rule and a
voice-matching mode. Its strongest five tells are structural, not lexical:
not-X-but-Y contrasts, one-line closers, "sayings that sound deep", staged
run-ups, and "arguing with no one". Then rhythm tells: forced triads, repeated
sentence openings, dashes used as a universal connector, stacked qualifiers.
This is nearly identical to what our own phase-0 and phase-1 measurements
found independently: the contracted corrective "it isn't X, it's Y" is our
strongest measured tell, and it is blader's rule 1. The skill bans em dashes
outright unless the writer's sample uses them. Its voice mode says a sample
"overrides the patterns below", matching the sample's dash rate rather than
zeroing it. That is a distribution target, not a floor, which is the one
design decision our phase-1 report argued for.

**Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing** (en.wikipedia.org, updated August 2026). The
source document. It is descriptive, not prescriptive, and it names the root
cause: models "regress to the mean", smoothing specific facts into generic
statements. Its most important warning is one our own research reached from the
other direction: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be
fixed; that could just make detection harder." Surface patching is not the
same as fixing prose, and it can be self-defeating. The page also carries the
detection-base-rate warnings: an untrained human is no better than random at
spotting AI text, while heavy LLM users reach about 90 percent (the Russell
result again).

**sam-paech/antislop-sampler**. A backtracking sampler that waits for a banned
phrase to appear in the output, then backtracks and down-regulates the tokens
that could have produced it. It handles thousands of phrases and supports
regex bans such as "not x, but y". It needs logit access, so it is open-weights
only. This is the inference-time sibling of the Antislop paper's FTPO method
already covered in our research brief.

**slop-forensics** (github.com/sam-paech/slop-forensics). Computes over- and
under-represented words and phrases in a model's output against a human
baseline (wordfreq), producing a per-model "slop profile", then infers model
lineage from profile similarity. It is the instrument behind the slop score
and it is why we can say, for example, that DeepSeek's later models converged
on Gemini-style slop.

**EQ-Bench slop score** (eqbench.com/slop-score.html). A combined metric: 60
percent slop-word frequency, 25 percent not-x-but-y pattern frequency, 15
percent slop-trigram frequency, normalized across models. The human baseline
scores 10.4. The best current model, claude-sonnet-4-5, scores 19.5, roughly
double human slop. Notably, one quarter of the metric is the not-x-but-y
pattern, independent confirmation that the tell we measured at 22 to 52 times
Alex's rate is a first-order feature of model prose.

**Unslop fine-tunes.** The evidence that the fine-tuning approach works is a
single model: `sam-paech/gemma-3-27b-it-antislop` scores 20.8 on the slop
score against its base model's 69.5. A 70 percent reduction in slop with a
surgical fine-tune. This matches the Antislop paper's FTPO claim of 90 percent
slop reduction while holding GSM8K, MMLU and creative-writing scores, and it
is the only intervention in this entire track with a clean, third-party
measured win. The cost is that it requires open weights, which means choosing
which model writes, a larger decision than a style tweak.

**Do the rule lists work?** The honest answer, from our own measurements: the
rules describe real tells, and the mechanical counters (slopcheck, slop score,
slop-forensics) measure them reliably. What the rule lists do *not* reliably
do is improve generated prose when handed to a model as instructions. Phase 1
showed a style prompt built from these exact measurements buys compliance on
the counts it names, pays for it in sentence-length variance and word count,
and inverts into a 39x vocabulary list on a weak model. The rules are a good
measuring instrument and a weak generation instrument.

### 4. The humanizer tax: does humanized text read worse

Yes, and the evidence is quantified.

DAMAGE's Fluency Win Rate is the direct measurement: even tier-1 humanizers
are preferred by a reader model only 26 percent of the time over the unmodified
AI text, and tier-3 tools 2.67 percent. "All humanizers tend to degrade the
quality of the original text." The degradation is not subtle. Tier-3 tools add
hallucinated citations, inline comments, and strings of question marks.
Tier-2 tools drop university-level prose to middle-school prose. Even tier-1
tools "occasionally misuse words due to dictionary lookup replacements"
(Twixify) or produce "flowery but slightly imprecise" text (Quillbot).

The tradeoff compounds the tax. A humanizer that degrades least is the most
detectable (Pangram's law). A humanizer that evades best is the one that
degrades most. So the tool cannot win on both axes, and the axis the tools
optimize, evasion, is the one that readers do not care about. Russell et al.
show the target reader catches humanized text anyway.

There is a separate, weaker question in the literature about whether *plain*
AI text reads worse than human text. The 2026 PLoS One study (Zou et al.)
found AI-generated abstracts had *lower* readability on eight metrics, meaning
more complexity, not less. That is not the humanizer tax, but it is a useful
correction to the folk model that AI text is "too simple". Our own phase-0 and
phase-1 data show the actual tells are structural (the corrective, triads,
dashes, one-line closers), not a uniformly low reading level.

### The two bins

Everything in this track sorts into the two bins the brief asked for.

**Bin A: change surface statistics to fool a classifier.** Synonym swaps,
tortured phrases, spacing and punctuation damage, homoglyphs, zero-width and
thin-space characters, deliberate typos and register collapse, perplexity and
burstiness injection, paraphrase-to-evade models such as DIPPER. Every one of
these degrades the prose or leaves a new tell, and the stronger detectors
(Pangram, DAMAGE, GPTZero, Originality) are already robust to them. Russell et
al. show the human reader is robust to them too. This bin is worse than
useless for our goal: it optimizes for the one audience, an automated detector,
that we do not care about, and it buys nothing with the audience we do.

**Bin B: make prose actually better and more personal.** Structural tell
removal aimed at the reader (blader/humanizer's 35 patterns, Wikipedia's signs,
our own slopcheck rules), fine-tuning that reduces the slop distribution at
the weight level (FTPO, the antislop gemma fine-tune), decoding changes that
restore the distribution tail (min-p, the antislop sampler), and mechanical
measurement of the author's own voice against a corpus (slopcheck). These do
not target detectors, they target the defect. They are the only interventions
in this track with measured wins, and they are the only ones that overlap with
what we already know works.

## Implications for our toolkit

1. **Do not build a "humanizer".** The commercial category is a dead end by
   our own criteria. It optimizes for detectors, degrades text, and fails
   against the reader we care about. Nothing in bin A is worth shipping.

2. **Detectors are a weak proxy for what readers notice, but a useful
   instrument.** The variance between detectors (Pangram near 100, Copyleaks 0
   on the same text) means no single detector score should gate a decision. If
   we measure against detectors at all, measure against the robust ones
   (Pangram, GPTZero) and treat Copyleaks-class tools as noise. The better
   instrument is the one that does not share the model's defect: a mechanical
   counter like slopcheck, slop-forensics, or the EQ-Bench slop score.

3. **The slop score's formula is a ready-made measurement target.** 60 percent
   slop words, 25 percent not-x-but-y, 15 percent trigrams, with a human
   baseline of 10.4. We can compute the same three quantities on Alex's corpus
   and set a two-sided target: reduce model slop toward his rate without
   undershooting it, because phase 0 showed his own writing trips generic slop
   lists.

4. **blader/humanizer is the closest existing thing to what we want, and it is
   a rule list.** Its value is the structural taxonomy and the no-invention
   rule. Its limit is exactly what phase 1 measured: rule lists moved counts
   but not distributions, and inverted on weak models. Adopt its taxonomy as a
   measuring instrument, not as a generation prompt.

5. **The one intervention with a clean win is FTPO on open weights.** It is the
   same conclusion our own research brief already reached, now independently
   confirmed by the antislop fine-tune's 69.5 to 20.8 slop-score drop. It
   remains gated on accepting a local model for drafting.

6. **Track meaning drift as a first-class check.** DAMAGE's audit found LLM
   humanizers add and delete claims. blader/humanizer spends a whole step on
   no-invention because a rewrite that improves style while changing facts is
   the failure mode. Any toolkit we build needs a fact-preservation check, and
   our own phase reports already warn that critique-and-revise loops with an
   LLM critic optimize toward the defect.

## Open questions

1. **Does fine-tuning help with the *structural* tells, or only the lexical
   slop words?** The EQ-Bench slop score weights lexical slop at 60 percent and
   the not-x-but-y pattern at 25. We do not have a clean measurement of whether
   FTPO-style fine-tuning reduces the corrective, triads and dashes that
   dominate our own phase-0 findings.

2. **The humanizer tax is measured with a model judge (GPT-4o Fluency Win
   Rate), not human readers.** DAMAGE is the best number we have, and it is
   still an LLM judge, which our own brief flagged as biased toward
   low-perplexity text. A human-reader study of humanized text quality is the
   missing piece.

3. **Does anything reduce slop *and* preserve the author's distribution at the
   same time?** Phase 1 showed a forbid list moves counts but collapses
   sentence-length variance. The antislop sampler and FTPO claim to fix this at
   the decoding or weight level, but neither has been tested against a
   stylometric baseline on a single author's corpus.

4. **Spanish.** Every instrument in this track (slop lists, slop score, the
   antislop phrase list, blader's patterns) is English. The profile method is
   corpus-driven and language-agnostic, but the corpus of Alex's Spanish
   writing in a consistent register still does not exist.

5. **Whether the "not-x-but-y" pattern that is 25 percent of the EQ-Bench slop
   score and our own strongest tell is actually reduced by any intervention.**
   It is the single most-corroborated tell across three independent sources,
   and nobody has published a measured reduction.
