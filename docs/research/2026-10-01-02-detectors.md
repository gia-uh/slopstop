# Track 2: How AI-text detection works in 2026, as an instrument

Research date: 2026-10-01. Scope: what detectors exist, what signal each
keys on, how they behave under attack and on mixed text, where they misfire,
and which ones we can run locally or call cheaply as a measuring instrument
for Alex's writing.

## Headline

1. **The detector market has split into two regimes that behave nothing alike.**
   Zero-shot likelihood methods (DetectGPT, Fast-DetectGPT, Binoculars) read
   token probabilities; trained classifiers (Pangram, GPTZero, Originality,
   open RoBERTa models) read style features end to end. The zero-shot methods
   hold up best at very low false-positive rates but their stability collapses
   under humanization. The classifiers are more robust to rewriting but their
   default thresholds are badly calibrated unless a vendor calibrates them.

2. **Any detector that reports a single 99% number is reporting at a high,
   undisclosed false-positive rate.** RAID shows the same detectors that hit
   99% accuracy at high FPR collapse to near zero when FPR is pinned at 1% or
   5%, and that several cannot even reach low FPRs (ZeroGPT plateaus at 16.9%,
   Fast-DetectGPT at 0.88%, Originality at 0.62%). A number without an FPR is
   not a measurement.

3. **Paraphrase and humanization are the single strongest lever against every
   family.** DIPPER drops DetectGPT from 70.3% to 4.6% accuracy at 1% FPR. A
   single self-edit prompt drops essay detection from 100% to 13%. Recursive
   humanification collapses the reliability of zero-shot detectors even while
   their headline AUROC stays high.

4. **Non-native formal technical prose is the exact false-positive risk group,
   and the risk is real but older than it looks.** Liang et al. measured 61.2%
   false positives on non-native English essays versus near zero on native
   ones, driven by low perplexity. A February 2026 revisit finds the effect is
   an artifact of the perplexity-based detectors of the 2023 era, not a
   property of modern classifiers. The safe stance is: do not trust any
   single detector's verdict on Alex's prose, calibrate against his corpus.

5. **Watermarking is now arriving (Gemini, and Anthropic's August 2026
   announcement for future Claude models) and it is irrelevant to us as an
   instrument.** It only lets the provider answer "was this partly written by
   us" using a key they hold. It reads nothing about style, it is stripped by
   any paraphrase, and no third-party detector uses it.

6. **For a measuring instrument, wire in two: one open classifier run locally
   (TMR or ModernBERT on Hugging Face, top of the RAID leaderboard) and one
   commercial API for the segment and AI-assist signal (Pangram, since it is
   the only vendor that returns per-segment fractions).** Use both as noisy
   thermometers calibrated on Alex's own corpus, never as a target to
   optimise against.

## Findings

### 1. Detector families and what signal each keys on

#### Zero-shot likelihood methods

These need no training data. They ask an open language model how "expected"
the text is, and flag text that is too expected as machine-written.

**DetectGPT** (Mitchell et al., ICML 2023, arXiv:2301.11305, Jan 2023) was the
first popular zero-shot method. It perturbs the text slightly and checks
whether the model's log-probability decreases under perturbation. Machine text
sits in a local maximum of log-probability, so small edits push it off the
peak. It is accurate but computationally expensive, because it requires many
perturbed passes.

**Fast-DetectGPT** (Bao et al., ICLR 2024, arXiv:2310.05130, Oct 2023) replaces
the perturbation step with a sampling step and defines a "conditional
probability curvature" metric. The paper reports roughly a 75% relative
improvement over DetectGPT in both white-box and black-box settings, and a
340x speedup. It uses a single reference model (GPT-J and GPT-Neo are the
defaults in the code). Its signal is the curvature of token log-probability in
context.

**Binoculars** (Hans et al., arXiv:2401.12070, Jan 2024, v3 Oct 2024) is the
current reference zero-shot method and the strongest at low false-positive
rates. It contrasts two closely related decoder models, an "observer" and a
"performer", and scores text by the ratio of its perplexity under the two. The
default pair is Falcon-7B and Falcon-7B-instruct. Over a range of document
types it detects over 90% of ChatGPT samples at a false-positive rate of
0.01%, without having seen ChatGPT data. RAID's authors confirm Binoculars is
"particularly strong at low FPR."

The shared signal across all three is the token-level likelihood surface:
perplexity, log-likelihood, token rank, and entropy. None of them look at
meaning, style, or n-gram patterns. That is exactly why they generalise across
generators (they never learned a particular model's style) and exactly why they
collapse under any rewrite that changes the token distribution.

#### Trained classifiers

These are supervised models trained on pairs of human and machine text.

**Open RoBERTa-style detectors on Hugging Face.** The classic reference is
OpenAI's own GPT-2 detector (`roberta-base-openai-detector`), which OpenAI
retired in mid-2023 because of low accuracy. Today the useful open models are
third party, and the RAID leaderboard is the cleanest way to rank them. Top
entries by aggregate AUROC on the non-adversarial leaderboard include:
TMR (Target Mining RoBERTa, `Oxidane/tmr-ai-text-detector`) at 0.997,
ModernBERT AI Detection (`GeorgeDrayson/modernbert-ai-detection-raid-mage`,
arXiv:2502.15654) at 0.991, ADAL (`Shushant/ADAL-detector-large`) at 0.980,
and a family of DeBERTa "ConPara" checkpoints around 0.97 to 0.99. These are
encoder-only models in the 100M to 500M parameter range, cheap to run on CPU
or a small GPU.

**GPTZero.** A commercial detector that reports perplexity and "burstiness"
(the variance of sentence-level perplexity; human writing is bursty, machine
writing is more uniform), combined with a trained classifier, sentence-level
highlighting, a "mixed" class for partial AI text, and multi-model detection
across Gemini, Claude, Llama, and others. It is aimed at educators.

**Originality.ai.** A commercial classifier targeted at content marketing and
publishing, reporting per-sentence scores and a probability.

**Pangram.** The most technically documented vendor. Its current model,
Pangram 4 (technical report dated July 2026), is a mixture-of-experts model
with about 6x the parameters of Pangram 3. It predicts three scores per token:
human-written, AI-assisted, and AI-generated, over overlapping 512-token
windows, and averages and smooths the results. It uses hard-negative mining
(retraining on human texts the model mislabeled, plus synthetic mirrors) to
reduce false positives. It claims 99.66% of AI documents caught at a 0.0041%
false-positive rate (about one human document in 24,000 flagged), and an
overall false-negative rate of 0.3396% across 26 model families including
GPT-5.5, Claude Opus 4.8, and Gemini 3.1 Pro. These are the vendor's own
benchmarks, on their own data, so treat them as claims, not independent
evidence. The architecture description is the useful part: it is the only
detector that distinguishes three classes (human, assisted, generated) and
returns per-segment boundaries.

Classifiers carry the decision on whatever the network learned, which spans
perplexity-like statistics and style and content features together. They are
better than zero-shot methods against paraphrase because a rewrite that
preserves meaning still preserves many surface habits the classifier learned
from.

#### Watermarking

**SynthID-Text** (Dathathri et al., Nature 2024, s41586-024-08025-4) is a
production watermarking scheme from Google DeepMind. It changes only the
sampling procedure (a "tournament" over a watermarking key), not the weights,
and its Nature paper reports no detectable quality loss, with a live
experiment on nearly 20 million Gemini responses. Detection is a statistical
test (a z-score against the key), not a classifier, and does not need the
model. It is open sourced.

**Which frontier models are watermarked in 2026?** Google has watermarked
Gemini output with SynthID-Text. Anthropic announced on 14 August 2026
(anthropic.com/news/claude-text-watermark) that future Claude models will emit
watermarked text, using a version of SynthID-Text, explicitly to comply with
the EU AI Act, whose marking requirement applies from 2 August 2026. The
Anthropic writeup is unusually clear about what the watermark can and cannot
do: it adds nothing to the text, costs no extra tokens, carries no identifying
information, and "one can only answer the question, what is the likelihood
this was partly written by Claude?" using a key that only the provider holds.
OpenAI has publicly discussed having a text watermark ready but has not
deployed one on ChatGPT text.

The point for this project: watermarking is a provenance mechanism, not a
style detector. It cannot be used as a measuring instrument from outside,
because the key is not ours and any humanization, paraphrase, or heavy edit
destroys the signal. It matters only as background: by 2026 the providers are
moving to make AI text provable at the source, which does not change anything
about how readers judge prose.

### 2. Robustness benchmarks

**RAID** (Dugan et al., ACL 2024, arXiv:2405.07940, May 2024; leaderboard at
raid-bench.xyz) is the standard. It spans 10M+ generations across 12 models,
11 domains, 12 adversarial attacks, and 4 decoding strategies, and it fixed
false-positive rate as the reporting convention. Its central findings:

- Detectors that claim 99% accuracy do so at high FPR. As FPR is reduced,
  accuracy collapses. Some detectors could not even reach a low FPR: ZeroGPT
  plateaued at a minimum achievable FPR of 16.9%, Fast-DetectGPT at 0.88%,
  Originality at 0.62%.
- Adding a repetition penalty to the generator drops detection accuracy by up
  to 38 points, across all detector families.
- Under adversarial attacks at FPR 5%, Binoculars loses as much as 36.1 points
  to synonym substitution. Homoglyph attacks cost most detectors an average of
  40.6 points, while GPTZero lost only 0.3.
- Default thresholds are dangerously mis-set: at the naive 0.5 threshold,
  open neural classifiers run false-positive rates well above 10% (a RoBERTa
  ChatGPT detector at 21.6%, Fast-DetectGPT at 47.3%) on human text. A detector
  must be calibrated on target-domain human text before deployment.
- Metric methods (Binoculars, Fast-DetectGPT) generalise best across unseen
  domains, better than classifiers, when no attack is present.

**DIPPER** (Krishna et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2303.13408, Mar 2023) is the
canonical paraphrase attack. It is an 11B paraphrase model with knobs for
lexical diversity and reordering. DIPPER drops DetectGPT's accuracy from 70.3%
to 4.6% at a constant 1% false-positive rate, and also defeats watermarking,
GPTZero, and OpenAI's text classifier. The paper's retrieval-based defense
recovers 80 to 97% of paraphrased generations at 1% FPR, but that defense
requires the generator's API provider to maintain a database of everything it
has ever emitted, which is why it is not a general instrument.

**SHIELD** (Ayoobi et al., arXiv:2507.15286, Jul 2025) is the most recent
benchmark and the one most relevant to humanizers. It adds a controllable
"humanification" framework with three strategies (random meaning-preserving
mutation, AI-flagged word swap, and a recursive humanification loop) and
evaluates detectors on a reliability-stability score rather than raw AUROC.
The headline: zero-shot detectors keep a healthy-looking AUROC under
humanification but their reliability and stability collapse. Fast-DetectGPT's
unified reliability-stability score falls from 61.8 on the paraphrase baseline
to 22.4 under recursive humanification; Binoculars falls from 40.9 to the
mid-teens. The adversarially trained supervised detector RADAR is the most
stable, holding up where zero-shot methods fail. The lesson is the same one
RAID drew: a high AUROC at easy settings is not evidence a detector works on
text that has been deliberately rewritten.

**Against humanizers specifically**, independent evidence is thinner. Pangram
claims 98.83% detection of AI involvement across 13 commercial humanizers, and
claims that a 24-hour red-team agent against its own API managed only one
repeatable false negative. Both are vendor statements and should be read as
marketing until an independent lab reproduces them. The Liang et al. self-edit
result is an older but independent demonstration of the same mechanism: a
single "elevate the language" prompt took essay detection from up to 100% to
13%, and scientific-abstract detection from up to 68% to 28%.

### 3. Detection of edited and mixed text

Only the commercial vendors seriously attempt this, and they do it by
returning fractions or segments rather than a single score.

**Pangram** returns the richest schema. Its API response includes
`fraction_ai`, `fraction_ai_assisted`, `fraction_human`,
`num_ai_segments`, `num_ai_assisted_segments`, `num_human_segments`, and a
list of `windows` with per-window labels, scores, and character ranges. For a
document where one paragraph is AI and the rest human, it reports the boundary.
For a paragraph that was iteratively rewritten so no token is cleanly
attributable, it flags the segment "AI-assisted." Pangram 4's own benchmark
claims it flags AI-polished human text as fully AI-generated only 0.009% of
the time, a 20x reduction over Pangram 3.

**GPTZero** reports a three-way human/AI/mixed classification plus
sentence-level highlighting, but does not expose the per-segment confidence
that Pangram does.

**How much editing flips a verdict?** The honest answer is that no independent,
calibrated number exists, and the available points cut in both directions.
Liang et al. showed that a single rewrite pass for vocabulary diversity cut
false positives on human text by 49 points and cut true detection of machine
text by up to 87 points, so a light edit can flip a verdict completely for
perplexity-based detectors. Pangram, from the other side, cites recent work
claiming it is hard to remove the signature of an AI draft even with heavy
editing, and that writers who edit LLM output toward their own style still
produce text that reads closer to the LLM than to themselves (arXiv:2604.24444,
cited in Pangram's report; also arXiv:2605.03202 on LLM-rewritten academic
prose accumulating hedging and emphasis words, and arXiv:2603.18161 on LLM
edits shifting meaning even when only grammar is touched). The practical
conclusion: the "how much editing" question is exactly what we can measure
ourselves with a detector wired in as an instrument, and it is currently
unsettled in the literature.

### 4. False positives

**Liang et al. 2023** (arXiv:2304.02819, Patterns, Apr 2023) is the study
everyone cites, and its numbers are stark. Seven detectors, tested on 91
non-native TOEFL essays and 88 native US 8th-grade essays: near-perfect
accuracy on native text, but an average false-positive rate of 61.22% on
non-native text. All seven detectors unanimously flagged 18 of 91 non-native
essays; at least one detector flagged 89 of 91. The mechanism is perplexity:
non-native writing has lower lexical and syntactic variability, so it looks
like low-perplexity machine text. They confirm this by finding that ICLR 2023
abstracts from non-native-English countries have significantly lower
perplexity than those from native-English countries, even after controlling
for review ratings.

The same paper's remedy is the warning for us: asking an LLM to "enhance the
word choices" cut the false-positive rate from 61.22% to 11.77%. Which means
the very tool we use to make prose read more natively is the thing that pulls
it into the detectable regime, and the thing that makes it less detectable is
also the thing that strips the author's voice.

**The February 2026 revisit** (Al Ali, Helcl, and Libovický,
arXiv:2602.05769, Feb 2026) complicates the story and matters for how we cite
it. In the Czech setting, they find non-native speakers have *higher* entropy
than natives, not lower; find no systematic bias against non-native speakers
across three detector families (bag-of-words ML, fine-tuned RoBERTa, and a
commercial detector); and conclude that contemporary detectors do not rely on
perplexity the way the 2023-era ones did. The honest synthesis: the 61%
figure was a property of a specific generation of perplexity-based detectors
(GPT-2 family, early GPTZero, OpenAI's classifier). Modern classifiers key on
learned features, not raw perplexity, so the bias has narrowed and shifted.
But two risk factors for Alex remain unchanged and are structurally the same:
he is a non-native English writer, and he writes formal technical prose,
which is low-variability by genre regardless of author. A detector calibrated
on general human text has no reason to know that low-variability formal prose
from a non-native author is still human.

### 5. What we can run locally or call by API

**Binoculars** runs locally. It needs two Falcon-7B models (Falcon-7B and
Falcon-7B-instruct), which is roughly 28GB of VRAM in 16-bit, comfortable on a
40GB A100 and loadable in 8-bit on a 24GB consumer card. BSD-3 licensed, free.
It is the strongest open detector at low FPR on RAID.

**Fast-DetectGPT** is lighter (a single GPT-J or GPT-Neo reference model) and
free, ICLR 2024, but its stability under rewrite is worse than the
classifiers'.

**Open classifiers** are the cheapest strong option. TMR
(`Oxidane/tmr-ai-text-detector`) and ModernBERT
(`GeorgeDrayson/modernbert-ai-detection-raid-mage`) sit at the top of the RAID
leaderboard and are encoder models that run on CPU or a small GPU. They return
a probability per document (and some support per-sentence scoring). Their
caveat from RAID: they need threshold calibration on human text in the target
domain before the number means anything.

**Pangram API** is $0.05 per 100 words, with a 20% discount on bulk jobs. It
is the only option that returns the three-way fraction and per-segment
breakdown, which is the signal that matters for our mixed-text editing
workflow. Terms: SOC 2, no training on customer or user-submitted data, zero
data retention available on enterprise. The free tier is 2,000 words per day,
which is enough to test.

**GPTZero API** starts at $45 per month for 300,000 words, then $150 per
million words overage, with a per-document cap of 150,000 characters. It
returns document-level and sentence-level labels plus the mixed class.

**Originality.ai** sells credits where one credit scans about 100 words, on a
pay-as-you-go model. It is oriented at publishers and reports per-sentence
scores.

## Implications for our toolkit

**Wire in two instruments, and only two.** A local open classifier (TMR or
ModernBERT) for a free, high-volume, private first pass, and the Pangram API
for the segment and AI-assist breakdown that no open model provides. Binoculars
is a worthwhile third cross-check if a GPU is handy, because it is the only
one that is independently strong at very low false-positive rates, but it is
not the workhorse.

**Always record FPR, and calibrate on Alex's corpus.** A raw detector score is
a likelihood under that detector's training distribution, and Alex's prose is
precisely the non-native formal-technical distribution those models were not
calibrated for. Before trusting any threshold, run the instrument over a
sample of Alex's published posts and over a matched set of his own topics, and
set the operating point there. This is the same design constraint Phase 0 hit
with `slopcheck`, and it applies unchanged to any external detector.

**Treat detector output as a probe of our own pipeline, never as a target.**
Every robustness result in this report says the same thing: a detector is a
noisy, gameable measure. If we optimise prose to lower a detector score, we
are optimising toward low perplexity and low variability, which is exactly the
defect we are trying to remove, and we would be rediscovering the Liang et al.
finding from the wrong side. The correct use is diagnostic: when a segment of
Alex's revised draft reads as machine-written, that is a lead to investigate,
not a number to minimise.

**The mixed-text signal is the highest-value measurement.** Pangram's
three-way fraction answers the question none of the likelihood methods can:
was this paragraph human-drafted and AI-polished, or AI-drafted and
human-edited? That distinction maps directly onto the two failure modes we
care about (over-polishing killing the voice, and a draft that was never
really ours), and no open model currently produces it. Spend the API money
there.

**Do not build around watermarking.** It will arrive on Claude, and it is
irrelevant to us: the key is Anthropic's, it reads nothing about style, and it
is destroyed by any paraphrase. It does, however, tell us that the providers
themselves are treating detection as a provenance problem at the source, not a
style problem at the reader, which is the same conclusion this research
reaches about what detectors are good for.

## Open questions

1. **The "how much editing flips a verdict" number is unsettled.** No
   independent study pins down, at a fixed FPR, how much human editing a
   detector can survive. This is the single most useful measurement for us and
   the literature does not have it. We should run it ourselves with the two
   instruments above on a controlled edit ladder.

2. **Whether Pangram's mixed-text and humanizer numbers survive independent
   reproduction.** Everything quantitative about Pangram 4 comes from Pangram's
   own benchmark and their own red-team exercise. We could not find an
   independent lab run against Pangram 4 specifically.

3. **Whether the non-native bias persists in current detectors for English
   technical prose.** The 2026 revisit tested Czech and found the bias gone;
   there is no equivalent recent English study, so we cannot rule out a
   residual effect on exactly Alex's register.

4. **Whether Binoculars with Falcon-7B remains a fair choice in 2026.** The
   pair is from early 2024, and its strength at low FPR was measured on 2024
   models. A modern open pair (for example a Llama-3 or Qwen pair) may score
   differently on current frontier output; nobody has re-benchmarked it on
   RAID against 2026 generators in the sources we read.
