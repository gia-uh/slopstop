# Track 3: what makes human readers decide a text is AI-written

## Headline

1. **Detection ability is bimodal, not general.** Lay readers run at or near random (56.7% true-positive rate in Russell et al., 51.7% false-positive rate, reported 2025). People who use LLMs daily for writing reach a 92.7% true-positive rate with 4% false positives, and their five-way majority vote gets 299 of 300 articles right. The gap is not general reading skill; it is familiarity with the specific vocabulary and shapes a model produces.

2. **The cue a reader chases predicts whether the call is right.** Novices flag "fancy words", perfect grammar, and a neutral tone, all of which are wrong heuristics and drive false positives. Experts flag a small set of actual overused words ("AI vocabulary"), formulaic sentence and document structure, and "optimistically vague conclusions". A 2026 survey of university professors ranks content-level tells (hallucinated facts, nonexistent sources) highest and "difficult words" lowest, the exact opposite of what lay readers do.

3. **Content-level tells are the ones a mechanical checker cannot see and the ones that survive all evasion.** The field guides and the detection studies agree on one mechanism: the model regresses to the mean, smoothing specific facts into generic, positive, exaggerated statements. Paraphrasing and "humanization" do not remove this. Affective flatness and emotional sameness persist as discriminators even after calibration.

4. **The disclosure penalty is real, large, and cannot be fixed by better prose.** Sixteen experiments and 27,491 participants show readers rate the identical text lower when told it is AI-written, across poetry, prose, first- and third-person, and human-versus-alien characters. Framing it as "human used AI as a tool" is penalized just as hard as full machine authorship. Perceived authenticity is the mediator, not text quality. This implies disclosure strategy, not humanization, is the only lever on the reader's reaction.

5. **There is almost no controlled evidence on the "one tell triggers a hunt for others" cascade.** What exists is indirect: confirmation bias is a documented reason human detection is unreliable, and the heuristics literature shows a single surface cue is enough to flip a judgment. This is the clearest gap for our own measurement.

## Findings

### 1. Human detection accuracy and the cues behind correct versus wrong calls

**Lay readers are near chance; expert users are near perfect.** Russell, Karpinska and Iyyer ([arXiv:2501.15654](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.15654), May 2025) hired annotators to read 300 paired non-fiction English articles (human-written versus GPT-4o, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, and o1-Pro) and label authorship with paragraph-length explanations. Four annotators who never use LLMs for writing detected AI text at a 56.7% true-positive rate with a 51.7% false-positive rate, which is random, while reporting high confidence (4.03 of 5). Five annotators who use LLMs daily for writing reached 92.7% true positives and 4.0% false positives. Their majority vote misclassified one article out of 300, an overall 99.3% true-positive rate at 0% false positives, tying the best commercial detector (Pangram) and beating every open-source detector. On the hardest setting, articles generated and humanized by o1-Pro, the expert vote was 100% while open-source Binoculars fell to 6.7% and Fast-DetectGPT to 23.3%.

**The same study names the cues that separate correct from wrong calls.** Novices fixated on "fancy" or low-frequency words as proof of AI, assumed humans are more grammatically correct than machines (the reverse is true), and read any neutral tone as AI, producing false positives. Experts recognized exact overused words (testament, crucial, significantly, vibrant) and also cited formulaic sentence and document structure and low originality. The most common single giveaway in the expert explanations was "AI vocabulary", followed closely by formulaic structure and "optimistically vague conclusions".

**The older studies set the baseline this updates.** Clark et al. ([arXiv:2107.00061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.00061), 2021) found untrained evaluators could not distinguish GPT-3 from human text in stories, news, and recipes at better than chance, and brief training raised accuracy to only 55%, not significant across domains. Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman ([PNAS 2023](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2208839120), 6 experiments, N=4,600) found participants could not detect AI-written self-presentations in professional, hospitality, and dating contexts, because they relied on flawed heuristics: they associated first-person pronouns, contractions, and family topics with human authorship, all of which a model produces on demand, letting AI write text perceived as "more human than human".

**A 2025 three-party Turing test shifts the framing to interaction.** Jones and Bergen ([PNAS 2025](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2524472123)) ran preregistered 5-minute conversations against a human and a machine. GPT-4.5, prompted to adopt a persona, was judged the human 73% of the time, more often than the real human participant; LLaMa-3.1-405B reached 56%. Without the persona prompt the same models dropped to 38% and 36%. Interrogators reasoned about stylistic and socio-emotional features, not about intelligence. The lesson for us: persona prompting, not raw model output, is what crosses the reader's threshold, and it crosses it precisely on social and stylistic cues.

**Newer academic-domain work converges on "modestly above chance".** Fiedler and Döpke (2025), cited in Wikipedia's own guidance, measured 57% recognition of AI text and 64% of human text on German theses. Georgiou ([MDPI AI in Education, 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/3042-8130/2/1/2)) surveyed university professors and found they rank hallucinated facts or explanations, nonexistent sources, and the absence of language errors as the most diagnostic features, with "use of difficult words" ranked lowest. This is the cleanest direct evidence that professional readers run on content, not vocabulary, while lay readers do the opposite.

### 2. The cue catalogues readers actually use in 2026

The richest and most current catalogue is Wikipedia's field guide, [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing) (last updated August 2026). It is descriptive, sourced, and organized by level. I group its cues by level, supplemented by the [Hacker News thread on it](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44700558) (July 2025) and the professor survey.

**Word level.** Overused "AI vocabulary", with the specific list shifting by era: 2023-2024 (delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, crucial, vibrant, underscore), mid-2024 to mid-2025 (align with, bolstered, fostering, showcasing, enhance), and mid-2025 onward (emphasizing, highlighting, showcasing) plus canned notability phrases. A few words are strong tells only in combination; one or two in isolation are noise. Wikipedia adds that this is the same signal Russell's experts named first.

**Sentence level.** Negative parallelisms ("not just X, but also Y", "not X, but Y", "Y rather than X"); the rule of three (adjective, adjective, adjective); avoidance of plain copulas, replacing "is" with "serves as / represents / functions as / boasts"; and overuse of transitions in the older era (Additionally, Notably, In summary). This maps directly onto our Phase 0 finding that the contracted corrective "it isn't X, it's Y" runs at 22-52 times Alex's rate.

**Paragraph and document level.** Undue emphasis on significance and legacy ("stands as a testament to", "marks a pivotal moment"); situating the subject in "broader debates"; outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects; section summaries that restate the core idea; a conclusion that reassures. Russell et al. call the same thing "optimistically vague conclusions".

**Argument and content level.** The guide's central claim is that the model regresses to the mean: it replaces statistically rare, specific, unusual facts with generic positive descriptions, so "the inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry", "simultaneously less specific and more exaggerated". It inserts judgment without quantification, a point made independently by an HN commenter: the tell is "a lot of judgement about the subject matter without quantification or comparison". Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage ("featured in Wired, Refinery29 and other prominent media outlets", "maintains an active social media presence") is flagged as more common in 2025-or-later models.

**Formatting level.** Overuse of em dashes, boldface, headings, tables, and Markdown; curly quotes; broken citation markup and hallucinated references. HN commenters independently volunteer the em dash and dash-family overuse as the first formatting tell they notice.

**What human writing looks like, as the inverse.** Wikipedia lists simple "is/has" phrases, plain synonyms (wrote not authored, used not utilized), superlative or definitive statements ("one of the best", "was the first"), and hedging qualifiers ("very", "perhaps", "tends to") as features more common in human writing. It also lists, as ineffective indicators, exactly the heuristics lay readers use: perfect grammar, "bland/robotic" prose, "fancy/academic" prose, and transition words in isolation.

### 3. Content-level versus style-level cues

**The content-level signal is the one that survives everything, and it is what a mechanical counter cannot see.** Three independent sources point at the same mechanism. Wikipedia's guide frames the root cause as statistical regression to the mean: smoothing specifics into generics that "could equally apply to many topics". Pagan et al. ([arXiv:2511.04195](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.04195), Nov 2025) built a computational Turing test over nine open models on X, Bluesky, and Reddit and found that even after fine-tuning, persona prompting, and context retrieval, a BERT classifier still separates AI from human text at 85% accuracy or higher, with affective tone and emotional expression the persistent discriminators. Instruction-tuned models were more detectable than their base models, and there was a trade-off between human-likeness and semantic fidelity. Sui's uncertainty-gap result, already in our research brief, is the mechanistic version of the same thing: aligned models suppress the uncertain tokens that carry specificity and surprise.

**Specificity and opinion are the missing variables, more than word choice, in the professional ranking.** Georgiou's professors put hallucinated facts, nonexistent sources, and absence of error at the top and vocabulary at the bottom. Russell's experts, who read carefully, cite vocabulary first but then name structure and "originality" as the second and third cues. The consistent reading is that word choice is the fastest signal for people who already know the vocabulary, but the durability of the signal, and what makes a text feel "off" to someone who does not know the vocabulary, is the absence of specific, personal, verifiable, opinionated content. This matters for our toolkit because the prior tracks are almost entirely style-side (word lists, em dashes, contractions), and the strongest evidence points at a content-side defect the current instruments do not measure at all.

**One caveat keeps this honest.** The idea that genericity is *the* tell is well documented in field guides and in affective studies, but I found no single controlled experiment that isolates "add specific verifiable detail" as an intervention and measures detection accuracy before and after. The Raj et al. disclosure study (below) varied story content (human versus alien characters, first versus third person) and found it did not move the disclosure penalty, which tells us content changes do not fix the reaction once authorship is known. Whether content changes fool an unaware reader remains unmeasured.

### 4. The disclosure penalty and AI aversion

**The penalty is large and the text is held constant.** Raj et al. ([JEP: General, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001889), reported via [PsyPost, April 2026](https://www.psypost.org/people-consistently-devalue-creative-writing-generated-by-artificial-intelligence/)) ran sixteen experiments with 27,491 participants over roughly two years. Participants read the same poems and stories and were told a machine or a human wrote them. Ratings dropped every time the machine was named, across poetry and prose, first- and third-person, and stories about humans versus aliens, animals, and robots. Changing evaluation instructions (art versus objective coherence) did not soften it. Priming participants with articles about machine capability or giving the program a name and gender did not soften it. Crucially, framing the work as "a human used AI as a tool" was penalized just as harshly as full machine authorship, even for award-winning human stories. The single strongest mediator was perceived authenticity, not perceived quality. Disclosure did not create ambivalence; it produced a straight negative shift.

**The penalty varies by genre, and it is worst where the human relationship is the point.** Nakano et al. ([arXiv:2510.24011](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.24011), IUI 2026) had 261 participants rate an author across six communicative acts before and after learning the degree of AI involvement. Disclosure eroded trustworthiness, caring, competence, and likability across the board, with the steepest declines in social and interpersonal writing. Higher AI literacy mitigated the effect. Sahebi et al. ([CHB: Artificial Humans, May 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2949882126000551)) found the same "AI penalty" in a 547-participant study comparing human, AI-assisted, and fully-AI authorship across workplace email and social media, and named the "disclosure paradox": people say disclosure is important, then penalize the communication when disclosure is made, creating an incentive not to disclose. For academic writing the picture is mixed: Li et al. (2024, cited in Nakano) found revealing AI involvement in essays lowered quality ratings, while other studies (Ayers et al. 2023, Ovsyannikova et al. 2025, cited in Nakano) find AI text rated equal or superior when efficiency and accuracy are what is valued.

**Implication for humanization.** Because the reaction tracks perceived authenticity, not text quality, no amount of style rewriting can remove the penalty once a reader believes or is told the text is AI-assisted. The lever that exists is the belief and the disclosure frame, not the prose. This is the strongest reason to treat "humanization" and "disclosure strategy" as separate problems with separate tools.

### 5. Does one tell trigger a hunt for others?

I found no controlled study that directly tests a cascade: trigger one suspicion, then measure whether readers find more tells. The evidence is indirect and points both ways. Wikipedia's guide warns, in its "Ineffective indicators" section, that confirmation bias and the Dunning-Kruger effect cloud human judgment, and that a false accusation of AI use drives away good editors, which implies readers do over-apply a suspicion once it forms. Jakesch et al. show that a single surface cue (first person, contractions, family topics) is sufficient to flip a judgment in either direction, which is the seed of a cascade. Russell et al. show that novices who fixate on the wrong first cue (fancy words) then make wrong calls, suggesting the first cue anchors the search. None of this is a controlled cascade measurement. Whether a single tell triggers a hunt, and which tell triggers it first, is our own measurement gap, and it is directly testable with a blind rating session and an eye-tracking or think-aloud protocol, or more cheaply with a two-stage rating design.

## Ranked list: cues most worth fixing

Ranked by the strength of evidence that they drive reader judgments, with an instrument column. "Mechanical" means a counter or regex sees it; "model" means a language model is needed to judge it; "human" means only a reader can assess it.

1. **Absence of specific, personal, verifiable detail and opinion (content).** Evidence: Wikipedia's core mechanism, Georgiou's professor ranking, Pagan's affective-discriminator result, Sui's uncertainty gap. Instrument: model (a critic that scores specificity) plus human. This is the top of the list because it is the signal that survives paraphrasing and humanization and the one our current mechanical instruments do not touch.

2. **Formulaic structure: optimistic-vague conclusions, significance/legacy padding, rule-of-three.** Evidence: Russell's experts name it second; Wikipedia documents it extensively. Instrument: mechanical for the conclusion/summary patterns, model for the semantic "does this sentence claim importance without evidence".

3. **AI vocabulary and phrase stock (the exact words and multiword phrases).** Evidence: Russell's experts name it first; Wikipedia tracks it by era; our own slopcheck confirms it against Alex's corpus. Instrument: mechanical (already built). This is the fastest signal for expert readers but is also the most movable by the model and the most time-sensitive, so it ranks below content.

4. **Negative parallelisms and the contracted corrective ("not X, but Y").** Evidence: Wikipedia documents it; our Phase 0 measured it at 22-52 times Alex's rate, the strongest single tell in our corpus. Instrument: mechanical.

5. **Avoidance of plain copulas and euphemistic inflation ("serves as" for "is", "utilized" for "used").** Evidence: Wikipedia's "signs of human writing" section and the copula-decline study it cites (over 10% decline in "is"/"are" in academic writing in 2023). Instrument: mechanical.

6. **Formatting tells: em dashes, boldface, heading density, Markdown.** Evidence: Wikipedia's style section and the HN thread both name the em dash first; Phase 0 measured em dashes at 2-5 times Alex's rate. Instrument: mechanical. This is cheap to fix and visible, but it is a weak signal for anyone who does not already suspect AI.

7. **Affective flatness and sameness of emotional tone.** Evidence: Pagan et al. show it is the most persistent discriminator. Instrument: model (sentiment/tone distribution) and human. Important for creative and personal writing, less so for technical prose.

8. **Word-level "fanciness" and neutral tone (the novice heuristics).** These drive false positives, not correct detection. Evidence: Russell's novices and Wikipedia's "ineffective indicators". They are worth *not* over-correcting for, because removing every "fancy" word and every neutral sentence is how human writing gets flattened into AI writing.

## Implications for our toolkit

**Measure content before style.** The current instrument (`slopcheck`) and the `unslop`/`rift` rules are entirely style-side: word lists, phrases, dashes, colons, headings. The evidence here says the durable signal is content-side: missing specifics, missing stakes, missing opinions, sentences that assert importance without evidence. Build a second instrument that scores specificity and evidence density against Alex's corpus, and a model-based critic that flags "claims importance without a supporting specific fact". This is the highest-value gap.

**Treat the disclosure reaction as unsolvable by prose.** The Raj et al. result, with 27,491 participants and constant text, is the closest thing to a settled finding in this space. Do not spend engineering effort trying to make AI-assisted prose survive a disclosure penalty. Spend it on the disclosure frame: when and whether to disclose, and how to communicate authorial agency (Nakano's design implication to "communicate human effort and agency" is the one directly actionable lever).

**Do not chase the novice heuristics.** The catalogues agree that "fancy words", perfect grammar, and neutral tone are wrong tells that drive false positives against real human writers. A toolkit that strips these from AI output will make AI prose less human, not more, which is the exact inversion the style-prompt experiment already measured on the weak-model forbid list.

**The cue catalogue is a moving target and should be data, not prose.** Wikipedia tracks the vocabulary by era (delve peaked in 2023-2024 and dropped off sharply in 2025). Any hard-coded word list rots. Keep the corpus-driven measurement as the source of truth and treat any static list as a snapshot with an expiry date.

**Persona is what crosses the threshold in interactive settings.** The Turing-test result (73% for persona-prompted GPT-4.5 versus 38% without) is the strongest positive evidence that a social/stylistic frame changes reader judgment, and it is the one place where a prompt-level intervention measurably works, in contrast to the negative prompt-level results in our own brief.

## Open questions

**The cascade is unmeasured.** I found no controlled study of whether one noticed tell triggers a hunt for others, or which tell fires first. This is directly testable and is the cheapest missing experiment.

**The specificity intervention is unmeasured.** No study isolates "add specific, verifiable, opinionated detail" and measures detection accuracy before and after. The content-side ranking above is supported by surveys and field guides and affective measurements, not by a clean causal experiment.

**Genre-specific disclosure penalties are coarse.** Creative and social writing show the largest penalty; technical and informational writing show the smallest, but the existing comparisons use different text types and different measures, so the ordering is directional, not quantitative.

**The penalty over time.** Raj et al. note the penalty persisted across two years but their sample was tech-skewed; whether it decays as AI writing becomes ordinary is genuinely unknown.
